30,000 Churches Across U.S. Band Together to Bring Young People Back Into the Fold

September 6, 2015 Christian

Nathan Schroeder poses in front of Highland Church in Plover, WI, Friday, Sept. 4, 2015. (Photo: Megan McCormick/Stevens Point Journal Media, Megan McCormick/Stevens Point Journal Media)

Christian churches in central Wisconsin are joining a nationwide initiative to bring people back into worship, especially the much-sought millennials who have left organized religion in droves.

National Back to Church Sunday is set for Sept. 20 and sponsored by nine Christian denominations and four Christian organizations including LifeWay Research and ChurchLeaders.com.

“More than 30,000 churches across the nation are participating because it gives people a way, a reason, to come back, or try a church for the first time,” said the Rev. Nathan Schroeder, pastor at Plover’s Highland Church, which is affiliated with the Assemblies of God Fellowship. Members are reaching out to friends, neighbors, coworkers or family to attend church that weekend, as Highland Church also offers services on Saturday, Schroeder said.

Millennials, people born between 1980 and 2004, represent the largest demographic in the nation with the potential to create enormous change in society, said Ann Herda-Rapp, an anthropology-sociology professor at the University of Wisconsin Marathon County.The latest estimate put the number of millennials at about 83 million people while the next largest demographic, baby boomers, has about 75 million people.

“Given their sheer size, the millennials, just like the baby boomers, will shape the workplace, consumption patterns and, thus, the economy and family patterns,” Herda-Rapp said.

Similarly, millennials also could breath new life into religion, she said. The downturn in church attendance nationwide often is attributed to boomers, who came of age during the social upheavals of the 1960s and ’70s and left mainline churches.

Appealing to the millennial generation is a natural fit for Highland Church, Schroeder said.

“My preaching style is to connect with those who live in the modern world, who don’t know the Bible or the terminology,” he said.

During the 1990s, the church’s membership was around 600 people, but it plummeted in the early 2000s. It has since rebounded to about 400 members, Schroeder said.

“It’s a slow but steady growth, in part, because of millennials,” he said.

According to a Pew Research Center survey released in May, about 60 percent of millennials don’t classify themselves as “a religious person.” The study of more than 35,000 Americans found that the Christian share of the U.S.population is declining, while the number of U.S. adults who do not identify with an organized religion is growing.
